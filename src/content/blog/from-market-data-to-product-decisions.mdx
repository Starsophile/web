## Reporting numbers is only the first step

A useful market analyst does more than describe what changed. The analyst clarifies why the change matters, what could explain it, what evidence is missing, and which decision should be considered.

This is the framework I use to structure smartphone-market research.

## 1. Define the decision

Start with a decision or question, not a dashboard. For example: is a product's price positioning clear, is a model losing attention earlier than expected, or is a price band underserved?

## 2. Establish the evidence

Document the source, time period, geography, unit, denominator and methodology. Separate observed facts from interpretations. If multiple sources disagree, investigate their definitions before averaging or choosing one.

## 3. Develop competing explanations

A decline in search interest might reflect lifecycle timing, a successor launch, promotions ending, or competition. Keep multiple explanations alive until the evidence can distinguish them.

## 4. Triangulate

Use more than one relevant signal where possible: search interest, shipments, retail sell-through, street prices, channel inventory, reviews and consumer research each measure different things. Agreement across independent signals increases confidence, but does not automatically prove causation.

## 5. Turn the finding into a decision

A recommendation should state:
- the action being proposed;
- the evidence supporting it;
- the expected benefit and trade-offs;
- the key uncertainty;
- the metric that would indicate success.

## 6. State confidence honestly

Label conclusions as observed, inferred, or hypothetical. A clear limitation is not a weakness; it tells a decision-maker what to validate before committing resources.

## The standard I want to meet

Every case study on this portfolio should help a reader understand the question, inspect the evidence, follow the reasoning, and challenge the conclusion. That is the difference between a polished chart and useful research.
